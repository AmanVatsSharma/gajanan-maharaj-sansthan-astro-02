# Gajanan Vijay Granth Parayan: The Complete Guide

When a devotee finishes reading the last adhyay of the Gajanan Vijay Granth, something quiet happens in the room. The closing verse dedicates the merit of the entire reading to all beings, and that act of outward dedication is precisely what gives the granth its power. For families who want to do a parayan at home — or understand what it means before they sit down to read at Shegaon — this guide covers the structure of the text, the different ways to do a parayan, the rules that shape the practice, and what devotees report gaining from it.

## What is the Gajanan Vijay Granth?

The Gajanan Vijay Granth is a devotional poem of twenty-one adhyays (chapters) composed in the Ovi meter by Shri Dasganu Maharaj, the foremost disciple of Shri Gajanan Maharaj. Written in Marathi, it recounts the life and teachings of Shri Gajanan Maharaj from the moment of his appearance in Shegaon through the many episodes that established him as a living saint. It is not a hagiography in the conventional sense — it is a spiritual text that uses the incidents of a life to convey the inner meaning of devotion, surrender, and the Guru's grace.

The title itself is significant. "Gajanan Vijay" means "the victory of Gajanan" — not victory over an external enemy but the inner victory of a consciousness that has surrendered to the Guru. The granth has been in continuous circulation among devotees since the early twentieth century, and parayan (continuous or structured reading) of the text remains one of the most common devotional practices in the Gajanan Maharaj tradition.

## Structure: 21 adhyays in the Ovi meter

Each adhyay covers a specific phase in the narrative. The first adhyay introduces the saint's appearance in Shegaon. The middle adhyays cover his daily life, the interactions with devotees, the various leelas that drew people toward him, and the teachings he offered in response to specific questions. The final adhyays cover the period approaching samadhi and the events immediately following.

The Ovi meter is the same meter used in the traditional Marathi abhang form, which gives the text its characteristic sing-song rhythm when read aloud. Devotees who do not read Marathi fluently often find that listening to a recorded parayan is the most accessible way to engage with the text — the rhythm carries meaning even when individual words are unfamiliar.

## Types of parayan

### Ekadashi parayan (1 day)

The simplest form: read all 21 adhyays in a single sitting, typically from early morning until the reading is complete. This is the most common form during special occasions — Samadhi Utsav, Pragat Din, or personal vow fulfillment. Families often begin at 4 or 5 am, read through the morning with breaks for tea and breakfast, and finish by early afternoon. The single-day format requires sustained attention, which is part of its spiritual purpose.

### Saptahik parayan (7 days)

Read 3 adhyays per day over seven days, or follow the traditional allocation where certain adhyays are grouped together. The seven-day format is the most popular for household parayan because it spreads the commitment across a week without requiring a full day free. Many families do this during the month of Shravan or before a pilgrimage to Shegaon. A saptahik parayan typically starts on a Monday or Thursday and concludes on the following Sunday or Friday.

### Navah parayan (9 days)

Nine-day parayan is less common but observed during specific vow fulfillments. The ninth day is often marked by a havan or special offering at the temple. This format is typically chosen when a devotee has made a specific sankalp (vow) related to a personal need — health, livelihood, or a family matter.

### Ekadashah parayan (11 days)

The eleven-day format reads roughly two adhyays per day and is the most detailed form of household parayan. It allows time for reflection on each section and is often accompanied by a short explanation or discussion among family members after each day's reading. This is the format most recommended for first-time parayan practitioners.

## Rules and preparation

**When to start:** Most traditional sources recommend beginning a parayan on an auspicious day — a Thursday (Guruvar), a Monday, or during the bright fortnight (shukla paksha). The Samadhi Utsav period (around Rishi Panchami) and Pragat Din (February 12) are the most common dates for starting a new parayan.

**Cleanliness:** The reading space should be clean. A fresh cloth is spread on the table or shelf where the granth is placed. Devotees bathe before beginning each day's reading, or at minimum wash their hands and feet.

**Seating:** A cushion or asana is placed for the reader. Some families keep a photograph of Shri Gajanan Maharaj or the Samadhi Mandir in view during the reading. This is a matter of personal practice rather than a strict requirement.

**Food:** Many devotees observe a partial or full fast on the concluding day. During the reading period, sattvic food is preferred — no onion or garlic on the day of reading, according to common household practice.

**Concluding the parayan:** After the final adhyay, the granth is wrapped in a clean cloth and stored respectfully. A havan or annadan at Shegaon is a common way to mark the completion. ## Benefits devotees associate with parayan

Devotees speak of several consistent outcomes. One is a quieting of the mind — the rhythmic reading creates a meditative state that many find difficult to achieve through other practices. Another is a sense of connection to the Guru that deepens over repeated parayan cycles. Many families report that starting a regular parayan (monthly or during specific months like Shravan) has become the spiritual anchor of their household.

The closing dedication of merit to all beings is worth special attention. In the Gajanan tradition, no spiritual practice is considered complete without this outward turn — the merit is not hoarded but released. This is consistent with the saint's own emphasis on selfless service and the Anand Sagar complex as a living expression of that principle.

## Frequently asked questions

**Q: Do I need to know Marathi to do a granth parayan?**
A: Not necessarily. Many devotees listen to recorded parayan in Marathi and find that the Ovi meter carries the meaning through its rhythm. Audio recordings are available through the Sansthan office and online. For household reading, you can use a Marathi edition with a Hindi or English translation alongside it.

**Q: How long does each day's reading take?**
A: One adhyay takes roughly 20 to 40 minutes depending on reading speed. A saptahik parayan (3 adhyays per day) takes about 90 minutes to two hours per session. Most families find this manageable alongside work and household responsibilities.

**Q: Can I do a parayan even if I have never done one before?**
A: Yes. There is no initiation or prerequisite. Begin by obtaining a copy of the granth (available at the Shegaon temple book stall or through authorized distributors), set aside a clean, quiet space, and read at your own pace. The first parayan is always the hardest — subsequent ones become increasingly natural.

**Q: What should I do if I miss a day?**
A: Continue from where you left off rather than trying to catch up by reading extra. Some devotees compensate by doing an additional day at the end of the parayan, but this is optional. Consistency of practice matters more than strict adherence to the schedule.

**Q: Is there a difference between reading the granth and reading about Shri Gajanan Maharaj's life?**
A: Yes, and both are valuable. The granth is a devotional text composed in verse by a realized disciple — its purpose is spiritual transformation through repeated contact with the Guru's leelas. Reading about the saint's life (such as this guide and our biography) provides intellectual context and factual understanding. Ideally, a devotee engages with both: the granth for the heart, the biography for the mind.
