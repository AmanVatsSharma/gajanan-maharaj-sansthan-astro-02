# Shegaon Aarti Timings and Daily Schedule

The day at Shri Gajanan Maharaj's Samadhi Mandir in Shegaon is built around **four aartis in a fixed sequence — Kakad before dawn, Madhyan around midday, Saptashringi as the evening session opens, and Shej at the day's close** — with darshan held in two sessions, morning and evening, and an afternoon break between them. Per the Sansthan's published guidance, the day opens for darshan around five in the morning with the Kakad Aarti, the Madhyan Aarti follows near half past eleven, the evening session reopens with the Saptashringi Aarti around four, and the Shej Aarti near half past nine sends the temple to rest. Exact times shift with the seasons and festival calendar, so the temple office's word — reachable through the [contact page](/contact) — is always final; this guide gives you the sequence, the character of each aarti, and how to plan a day around them.

## The four aartis at a glance

| Aarti | Where it sits in the day (per published guidance) | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Kakad | Pre-dawn, around 5 AM; opens the morning darshan session | The waking of the temple; hushed, lamp-lit, the day's most intimate crowd |
| Madhyan | Late morning, near 11:30 AM; gathers the morning session toward its close | Fuller hall; families timing darshan before the afternoon break |
| Saptashringi | Around 4 PM; opens the evening darshan session | The town returning from its day; energy rising |
| Shej | Around 9:30 PM; closes the evening session | The putting-to-rest; the quietest large gathering of the day |

Between the two sessions, the temple observes an afternoon break — published guidance places the morning session from about 5 AM to midday and the evening session from about 4 PM to 10 PM. The companion piece to this article, our [Shegaon darshan timing guide](/blog/shegaon-darshan-timing-guide), holds the fuller darshan picture; this guide concentrates on the aartis themselves and on shaping a pilgrim's day around them.

## Kakad: the temple wakes before the town

The Kakad Aarti is the day's threshold. In the pre-dawn dark the hall is at its least crowded and most concentrated — devotees who have taken an overnight train from Mumbai, Pune or the Nagpur side arrive straight into it, and families staying in the Bhakta Niwas blocks walk over while the lanes are still quiet. The aarti itself belongs to the genre of mangala aartis across Maharashtra's temple culture: the waking of the deity with lamp, song and gently building sound, as if the temple were a household and the saint its householder.

First-time visitors are often told to make Kakad the one non-negotiable of their itinerary, and the counsel is sound. The crowd is manageable, the air is cool even in April, and the contrast with the same hall at midday teaches more about the temple than any description. Families with elders should note the practicalities: the walk in the dark, the cooler hour to stand in, and the room-to-temple distance that matters more at that hour than any other — the [Shegaon Bhakta Niwas page](/locations/shegaon-bhakt-niwas) carries the placement details.

The Kakad hour also has its own arrival arithmetic. Shegaon's overnight trains from the Mumbai and Pune side and the late-night connections from the Nagpur direction deliver a substantial share of each day's pilgrims in the small hours, and a family that plans for this arrives refreshed instead of stranded: the lighter bag with pooja items and fresh clothes kept accessible, the heavier luggage left with the reception as guided, and the room — which under the 24-hour cycle counted from entry will be held from whatever hour you take it — requested with the early arrival noted. Devotees regularly report that the Kakad they attended on arrival morning, half-sleepless and singing quietly, remains the aarti they remember from the whole trip. The room-request mechanics are covered in the [Bhakta Niwas complete booking guide](/blog/bhakta-niwas-complete-booking-guide).

## Madhyan and the shape of the morning

The Madhyan — noon — aarti gathers the morning session toward its close, and the hall is fuller: pilgrims who came in through the forenoon, local families, and the group tours that time their arrival to the late morning. The mood is less hushed than Kakad's but more communal; this is the aarti at which a visitor most feels the temple as the town's drawing room.

The useful planning fact about Madhyan is what follows it. The afternoon break between the sessions — roughly midday to four, per the published pattern — is not dead time but the day's natural intermission, and a family that plans for it converts a long day into two comfortable halves. The classic intermission pattern: return to the room for rest and a meal; or, for households with energy to spend, the Anand Sagar complex — developed by the Sansthan as gardens and lake — makes the natural afternoon excursion, as our article on the [spiritual importance of Anand Sagar](/blog/spiritual-importance-of-anand-sagar) explains.

The midday meal deserves its own sentence in any schedule built around the aartis, because in this tradition eating is part of the temple's day rather than an interruption of it. The mahaprasad served at Shegaon is the annadan the saint's own leelas centre on — the kitchens that tradition says could not be emptied — and the noon hour, between the Madhyan Aarti and the afternoon break, is when most families take it. Treat the meal as devotional time rather than logistics: seated rather than hurried, taken with the party rather than in shifts, and — for the children of the family — the hour at which the temple makes its most direct argument. What is served, when and how, is covered in our [mahaprasad and food guide](/blog/mahaprasad-and-food-guide).

## Saptashringi and Shej: the evening session, opened and closed

The evening session opens around four with the Saptashringi Aarti, and from that point the temple runs at its most social volume through the early night. This is the aarti families with children most easily join — the hour is kind to small routines, the hall is full but moving, and the singing carries the excitement rather than fighting it. Devotees making a one-day trip typically anchor their visit to this session, arriving by mid-afternoon and leaving after the early evening.

The Shej Aarti near half past nine is the day's putting-to-rest — shej is, simply, sleep. The crowd thins to the devoted core, the tempo drops, and the sequence of lamp and song has the character of a household closing its doors. Pilgrims who stay for Shej and then walk back through the emptying lanes describe it as the aarti that stays with them longest. For etiquette at any of the evening gatherings — where to stand, how to hold the plate, what to do with prasad — our [evening aarti participation guide](/blog/evening-aarti-participation-guide) is the practical companion.

## The aarti as practice, not spectacle

A caution worth an article-section of its own: the four aartis reward participation over attendance. It is entirely possible to stand through all four in a day as a spectator, camera-adjacent and unmoved, and go home with photographs. The tradition's own design works differently. The aarti is a fixed sequence of movements — the lamp, the song, the offering, the shared prasad — and each movement is an invitation: the refrain exists so the hall can carry it, and the prasad exists so the devotee's hands do something, not so they watch something being done. Devotees who arrive knowing even the refrain of the aarti — it is short, and our [aarti and mantra guide](/blog/gajanan-maharaj-aarti-mantra-lyrics-meaning) teaches it — report a categorically different experience from those who arrive cold. Singing quietly, accepting the prasad deliberately, standing through the whole of the sequence even when the legs complain: these small acts of joining are what the day is made of. The temple does not need your voice; the practice does.

The fourfold rhythm also teaches something households take home. A family that has lived one full Shegaon day — waking, noon, evening, sleep, each with its aarti — has experienced a complete cycle of remembrance, and the pattern scales down: the same rhythm lives in a household shrine at a fraction of the scale, morning lamp and evening lamp with the day's work between. This is why devotees speak of the aarti schedule less as information and more as formation: the day itself is the teaching.

## Shaping a day around the sequence

1. **Choose your anchor aarti first.** Kakad for depth and quiet, Saptashringi for family ease, Shej for the day's most poetic hour — the rest of the plan follows from this one choice.
2. **Match the room to the anchor.** Under the 24-hour check-out cycle counted from entry, your arrival hour becomes your departure hour; arriving the evening before a Kakad plan gives you the pre-dawn walk without a pre-dawn journey. Requests go through the [booking page](/booking).
3. **Split the day at the afternoon break.** Two halves with a rest between beats one long march, especially with elders or children in the party.
4. **Hold one plan loosely.** Aarti attendance needs no registration; the sequence runs every day of the year. What wants planning is the day around it, not access to it.
5. **Confirm the day's times at the office.** Seasons, ekadashis and utsavs shift the pattern — the office's guidance, via the [contact page](/contact), outranks any article including this one.

## What changes on festival days

On ekadashis, the Shravan Mondays, the February jayanti window and above all the Gajanan Vijay Utsav of Bhadrapad — covered in our [punyatithi guide](/blog/gajanan-maharaj-punyatithi-guide) — the sequence stands but everything around it inflates: queues begin earlier, the sessions stretch, and the aarti crowds multiply. The same caution in reverse applies to quieter weeks: ordinary weekdays of the off-season let a family stand within a few rows of the sanctum at every aarti of the day. The seasonal arithmetic of all of this is mapped in our [chaturmas guide](/blog/gajanan-maharaj-chaturmas-guide) and the [official Sansthan guide for devotees](/blog/gajanan-maharaj-sansthan-complete-guide).

## Frequently asked questions

### How many aartis are held at the Samadhi Mandir each day?

Four, in a fixed sequence: Kakad before dawn, Madhyan late in the morning, Saptashringi as the evening opens, and Shej at the day's close — with darshan held in two sessions around them and an afternoon break between.

### What are the timings of the aartis?

Per the Sansthan's published guidance, Kakad is around 5 AM, Madhyan near 11:30 AM, Saptashringi around 4 PM and Shej near 9:30 PM, with the morning session running to about midday and the evening session from about 4 PM to 10 PM. These move with seasons and festivals — confirm your date with the temple office through the [contact page](/contact).

### Which aarti is best for a first visit?

Kakad, if the family can make the pre-dawn hour; Saptashringi if children or elders make the evening kinder. The two deliver the same sequence in different registers — the hushed waking and the full-voiced evening.

### Do we need to register to attend an aarti?

No. Aarti attendance at Shegaon requires no prior registration; devotees simply join. Planning effort belongs to the day around the aartis — rooms, travel and the afternoon break — rather than to access.

### Where can we learn the aarti beforehand?

The aarti and the "Gan Gan Ganat Bote" mantra, with text, transliteration and meaning, are presented in our [aarti and mantra guide](/blog/gajanan-maharaj-aarti-mantra-lyrics-meaning). Learning the refrain before travelling turns attendance into participation.
