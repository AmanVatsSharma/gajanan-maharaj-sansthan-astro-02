# Importance Of Sankalp Before Yatra | Spiritual Guidance

The morning she decided to go to Shegaon, Priya did not make a list. She did not book a ticket, pack a bag, or check the darshan timings. She sat on the floor of her flat in Pune, lit a single diya, and said out loud: I am going to Shegaon to ask for my mother's health. That was her sankalp — the deliberate, spoken intention that transformed a vague wish into a sacred commitment. What followed was a six-hour drive, a night at Bhakt Niwas, a darshan at the Samadhi Mandir where she stood in queue with that single thought repeating in her mind, and a prasad she describes as the first thing in months that made her feel calm. Sankalp does not change the logistics of travel, but it changes the traveller. This guide explains the spiritual and practical significance of sankalp before beginning a pilgrimage, with specific reference to the Shegaon tradition, and offers a checklist you can use before any visit to the Sansthan.

## What sankalp means in the Hindu tradition

Sankalp is a Sanskrit word formed from two roots: sam, meaning "together" or "good," and kalpa, meaning "will" or "resolve." Together, sankalp means a well-formed intention — not a casual wish, but a deliberate, conscious decision to direct one's energy toward a specific purpose. The concept appears throughout the Dharmashastra literature, where sankalp is described as the mental act that consecrates a ritual, giving it meaning beyond its physical form. A puja performed without sankalp is a physical gesture; a puja performed with sankalp is a spiritual act.

In the context of pilgrimage, sankalp serves as the bridge between the desire to visit a sacred site and the actual journey. It is the moment when the pilgrim moves from thinking about going to deciding to go, and from deciding to go to committing to the purpose of the journey. Without sankalp, a yatra can become tourism — a sequence of destinations checked off a list. With sankalp, the same journey becomes tapas — a disciplined spiritual practice with a clear inner objective.

## Why intention matters more than ritual

Shri Gajanan Maharaj's teachings, as recorded by Dasganu Maharaj in the Gajanan Vijay Granth, repeatedly emphasise the primacy of inner state over outer form. The famous episode where Maharaj accepted a simple piece of jaggery from a poor farmer while turning away an elaborate offering from a wealthy merchant is often cited as evidence of his rejection of ritual excess in favour of genuine devotion. The lesson extends directly to pilgrimage: a devotee who arrives at Shegaon with a pure intention but a worn-out shirt is closer to the spirit of Maharaj's teaching than a devotee who arrives with expensive offerings but a distracted mind.

Sankalp is the mechanism that brings intention to the surface. By articulating your purpose — whether it is a prayer for a loved one's health, gratitude for a blessing received, or a simple desire for spiritual clarity — you create a mental and emotional anchor that sustains you through the inevitable difficulties of pilgrimage: the crowded queues, the long train journeys, the unfamiliar food, the exhaustion. When the going gets hard — and it always does on a multi-day yatra — the sankalp is what reminds you why you came. The [Significance of Temple Stay in Devotion](/blog/significance-of-temple-stay-in-devotion) discusses how carrying this inner purpose into your accommodation routine deepens the entire experience.

## A practical sankalp ritual before leaving home

The most effective sankalp practice is simple, brief, and repeated. Before you leave your home for Shegaon or any other destination, sit quietly for five minutes in a space where you will not be interrupted. Light a diya or candle if you have one. Speak your sankalp out loud in your own words — not in Sanskrit verses you have memorised, but in the language you think in. State where you are going, why you are going, and what you are asking for or offering. Then commit to one small act of service or devotion during the journey — it might be volunteering at the Mahaprasad kitchen, distributing prasad to fellow travellers, or simply maintaining a silent prayer for someone who is suffering. This spoken commitment transforms the yatra from a personal holiday into a spiritual practice, and it sets the tone for everything that follows.

## Sankalp at the temple — the moment of first darshan

The first darshan at Shegaon is, for most devotees, an overwhelming experience. The queue moves slowly, the temple acoustics carry the sound of bhajans and aarti bells simultaneously, and the moment of standing before the Samadhi Mandir arrives faster than expected. The instinct is to rush through the darshan — a quick namaskar, a moment of prayer, and then moving on to the next item on the itinerary. Sankalp changes this default. Before entering the main queue, pause at the entrance, close your eyes for ten seconds, and silently restate the sankalp you made at home. This brief act of recollection — this conscious return to your original intention — transforms the darshan from a visual experience into a devotional one. The [Pragat Din Darshan Planning guide](/blog/pragat-din-darshan-planning) offers specific timing advice for major festival darshans, where the queue pressure makes this pre-darshan pause even more important.

## Different types of sankalp — personal, family, gratitude, and healing

Sankalp takes different forms depending on the pilgrim's situation. A personal sankalp might be a specific prayer for guidance in a career decision or a difficult relationship. A family sankalp might be offered on behalf of a sick relative, a child's education, or the well-being of the entire family unit. A gratitude sankalp — offered after receiving a blessing — is equally powerful: rather than asking for something, you are acknowledging what you have already received and committing to honour that gift through your actions. A healing sankalp, offered for someone who is unwell, carries particular emotional weight and requires the pilgrim to sustain that intention through the physical demands of the yatra. There is no hierarchy among these types — the Gajanan Maharaj tradition accepts all sankalps equally, because the underlying principle is the same: a human being turning their heart toward the divine with clarity and sincerity.

## Sankalp for multi-location yatras

When planning a pilgrimage that visits multiple temples — Shegaon, Omkareshwar, Pandharpur, and Trimbakeshwar, for example — the sankalp practice becomes layered. Each destination warrants its own sankalp, specific to that temple's energy and your purpose in visiting it. At Shegaon, your sankalp might focus on gratitude and the teachings of Shri Gajanan Maharaj. At Omkareshwar, set a sankalp around the Narmada River's purifying quality. At Pandharpur, align your intention with Lord Vitthal's promise of unconditional acceptance. At Trimbakeshwar, set a sankalp connected to the Godavari's origin and Lord Shiva's transformative energy. The [Multi-Location Pilgrimage Planning Guide](/blog/multi-location-pilgrimage-planning-guide) covers the logistics of visiting these four locations in a single itinerary, but the spiritual architecture of that journey is built on these individual sankalps.

## How sankalp transforms difficult experiences

Crowds are the most common source of complaint among Shegaon pilgrims, and they are also the most common spiritual teacher. A devotee without sankalp stands in a three-hour queue at Pragat Din and experiences frustration, heat, and impatience. A devotee with a clear sankalp — standing in that same queue on behalf of someone who could not be there — experiences the wait as a form of penance and dedication. The physical conditions are identical; the inner experience is completely different. This is the transformative power of sankalp: it reframes difficulty as devotion. The same principle applies to long train journeys, basic accommodation, unfamiliar food, and the inevitable small frustrations of any multi-day trip. Sankalp does not remove the difficulty; it removes the suffering that difficulty produces when it is experienced without purpose.

## A sankalp checklist for your Shegaon visit

Before any visit to Shegaon, run through this brief checklist to anchor your pilgrimage in intention:

1. Define your primary purpose in one sentence — write it down if it helps.
2. State your sankalp out loud, even if you are alone.
3. Commit to one act of service during the visit (kitchen seva, prasad distribution, or simply sitting with another pilgrim who looks lonely).
4. Identify one moment for gratitude — the first darshan, the Mahaprasad meal, or the Anand Sagar evening — and protect time for it in your schedule.
5. Before entering the Mandir for darshan, pause at the entrance and restate your sankalp silently.
6. After leaving Shegaon, write down one insight from the visit before the feeling fades.

This practice takes less than five minutes on either end of the journey and requires no equipment, no guide, and no external validation. It is entirely yours.

## The psychology of sankalp — why spoken intention changes behaviour

Neuroscience offers a useful parallel to the sankalp tradition. When you articulate a goal out loud — rather than merely thinking it — the brain encodes it more deeply through a process called encoding specificity. The motor act of speaking engages additional neural pathways, and the social act of making a commitment (even to yourself) activates the brain's accountability circuits. In practical terms, a pilgrim who has spoken their sankalp out loud before leaving home is significantly more likely to return to that intention when the going gets hard — when the queue stretches, when the hotel room is not what was expected, when the train is delayed by six hours. The sankalp becomes a mental touchstone, and returning to it is a form of self-regulation that reduces stress and sustains motivation.

The parallel with cognitive behavioural therapy is not coincidental. CBT practitioners routinely ask patients to articulate their values and intentions as a way of building psychological resilience, and the sankalp tradition arrives at the same practice through a spiritual rather than clinical framework. The difference is that sankalp does not merely help you cope with difficulties — it reframes them. A CBT patient might learn to tolerate a difficult queue by focusing on their breathing. A pilgrim with a sankalp experiences the same queue as an opportunity to practice patience on behalf of someone else. The outcome — reduced distress — is similar, but the inner meaning is richer.

For devotees travelling with children, modelling sankalp is one of the most effective spiritual teaching tools available. Watch a parent speak their sankalp out loud before darshan, and a child absorbs the lesson that pilgrimage is not a sightseeing trip but a conscious spiritual act. Children who grow up with this practice carry it into adulthood, and many adult devotees trace their commitment to regular temple visits to a single moment in childhood when they watched a parent pause before entering a Mandir and speak a quiet intention.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to know Sanskrit to make a sankalp?**
Not at all. Sankalp is most effective when spoken in your own language, in your own words. The power lies in the clarity of your intention, not in the complexity of the language. A simple, heartfelt statement in Marathi, Hindi, English, or any language you are comfortable with is perfectly appropriate.

**Can I make a sankalp for someone else who cannot visit Shegaon?**
Yes, and this is one of the most common forms of sankalp at Shegaon. Many devotees visit on behalf of sick relatives, elderly parents who cannot travel, or friends who have asked them to pray. State the person's name in your sankalp and carry their photograph or a small memento if it helps you maintain the connection during darshan.

**Should my sankalp change for each temple on a multi-location yatra?**
Ideally, yes. Each sacred site carries a different energy and historical significance, and aligning your sankalp with each location's specific character deepens the experience. At Shegaon, focus on Shri Gajanan Maharaj's teachings of service. At Omkareshwar, the Narmada's purifying quality. At Trimbakeshwar, Lord Shiva's transformative presence. Details for each location's darshan timings and traditions are covered in the [Omkareshwar Darshan Timings guide](/blog/omkareshwar-darshan-timings).

**Is there a specific time of day that is best for making sankalp?**
The traditional recommendation is early morning, before the day's activities begin, when the mind is fresh and the world is quiet. At Shegaon, many devotees make their sankalp at the 5:30 AM aarti, standing in the queue with the early-morning stillness around them. Whatever time you choose, consistency matters more than timing — the sankalp grows stronger with repetition.

**What if I forget my sankalp during the yatra?**
This is completely normal and nothing to worry about. Pilgrimage is demanding, and the mind wanders. When you notice that you have lost touch with your original intention, simply return to it — gently, without self-criticism. A sankalp is not a test you pass or fail; it is a compass you consult when you need direction. The moment of remembering is itself a moment of spiritual practice.

**Can sankalp help with practical concerns like accommodation and travel?**
Indirectly, yes. A clear sankalp reduces the mental noise that makes logistical problems feel overwhelming. When your purpose is clear, the practical challenges of finding accommodation, navigating transport, and managing a tight schedule become problems to solve rather than obstacles that define the experience.
